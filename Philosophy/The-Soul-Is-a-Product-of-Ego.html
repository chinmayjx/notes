The Soul Is a Product of Ego

The Soul Is a Product of Ego

Oct 23, 202618 min read

  • philosophy
  • soul
  • consciousness
  • ego

Why the usual picture of the soul looks like the ego’s handiwork, and why I still believe in a soul anyway

Ask most people what a soul is and you get roughly the same picture. There’s a light inside the body. It’s what makes the body alive, and when it leaves, the body is just a body. Each of us has one. It carries who we are, and in many traditions it goes on after we die.

I’m going to argue that this picture was made by the ego. Then, at the end, I’ll tell you why I still believe in a soul. Just not that one.

A name in the sand

I can’t prove this, so let me be clear about what kind of claim it is.

Say you’re walking on a beach and you find a name written in the sand. It’s possible that wind and waves happened to leave that exact pattern. You can’t strictly rule it out. But nobody believes it. The far likelier story is that someone was here with a stick. You don’t need to have seen them. The pattern gives them away, because it looks like the kind of thing people make.

A fairer version

A name is almost impossible to get by accident, so it makes my case sound stronger than it is. A fairer example is a face. Nature does make faces sometimes: the famous “face on Mars” turned out to be a hill and some shadows, and people see faces in clouds and on the Moon all the time (psychologists call this pareidolia). So a face in the sand isn’t proof. But if you found one on a beach, you’d still bet on a person. That’s the strength of my claim: not proof, a strong lean.

The usual soul has a pattern like that. In What Is Ego? I described ego as the I-maker: the part of the mind that takes this body to be me, and then tilts everything in its favour. Now look at the soul. There’s exactly one per body. It’s mine. It carries my memories and my personality. It’s what makes me special among animals. And in many versions it survives my death, which is the one thing the body can’t do.

That’s the shape the ego would draw if it were drawing a soul.

I can’t make a logical case against something that, by definition, isn’t made of anything we can measure. What I can do is show why the odds lean one way. The rest of this piece is that.

A default, not a conclusion

First, notice that almost nobody reasons their way to a soul. It’s there before any reasoning starts.

Young children, in every culture psychologists have studied, assume that a person has a mind or spirit that’s separate from the body, without anyone teaching them. Psychologists call this intuitive dualism. It’s one of the defaults we’re born with, like the way people all over the world smile the same way when they’re happy.

Defaults like this have explanations. For a social animal, it pays to treat yourself and others as someone inside, with plans and feelings, because that’s how you predict what they’ll do next. So the feeling that there’s a light inside is exactly what a brain built by evolution would produce, whether or not the light is really there. That doesn’t make the feeling wrong. It does mean the feeling isn’t evidence.

So let’s look at the picture itself.

Blurring the boundary of the body

What does it even mean for the soul to be in the body? Two things seem to define “inside”: what I can control, and what I can feel.

Control is the easy one. I can move my hand. I can also move the cup on that table; it just takes a few more steps. The difference is distance, and distance is only one of the limits matter puts on me. I can’t move a cup in the United States from here. But I also can’t bend my hand backwards, and that hand is supposedly full of soul. Matter has its limits inside the skin and outside it. Control doesn’t stop at the skin in any special way.

Feeling is the stronger case. Pinch the cup and I feel nothing. Pinch my hand and I do. Surely that’s where I end?

Go one level down and look at what feeling is. Every experience is a chain of changes passing through matter. Light leaves the sun, bounces off the cup, enters the eye, becomes a signal in a nerve and reaches the brain. A pin presses the skin, a signal travels up a nerve and through the spinal cord, and reaches the brain. The hand is one link in a chain, just like the cup. Nothing special happens at the skin. The chain just passes through it.

Where you feel something is a separate question, and the brain decides it. It places every sense somewhere. Sound seems to be out there, where it came from. Sight comes with depth, laid out across the room. Touch and pain get placed on a map the brain keeps of the body. So touch isn’t the soul filling the body. It’s one sense among several, and its map happens to be shaped like the body. The others use a map of your surroundings.

That map lines up with the body, for obvious practical reasons. But it isn’t tied to the flesh, and you can show it.

  • The rubber hand illusion. Hide someone’s real hand behind a screen and put a rubber hand in front of them. Stroke both with a brush at the same moment. After a minute or two, many people start to feel the brush on the rubber hand. Then bring a hammer down on the rubber hand. People flinch, and their stress response jumps, as if it were their own.
  • Phantom limbs. Many people who’ve lost an arm or a leg still feel it, and some feel pain in it for years. The map kept a hand the body no longer has.
  • A numbed hand. After a nerve block, or a spinal injury, a hand can be completely alive: fed by blood, warm, able to heal. And it drops out of experience entirely.

That last one goes straight at the picture we started with. “Alive” and “felt” come apart. The light that supposedly makes the body alive and the light of experience aren’t the same thing, and neither fills the body edge to edge.

So whatever experiences, it isn’t spread through the body like light through a lamp. It’s concentrated wherever the maps are kept, and the edge of the body is one of the things it projects. The skin isn’t the soul’s border. It’s the ego’s.

A single point?

The obvious retreat is: fine, the soul isn’t body-shaped. It’s a point. The one place where everything comes together, where I see and I decide. Philosophers have tried exactly this; one famous attempt put the soul in a single small gland in the middle of the brain.

So let’s test the point.

Where does the line fall?

Start with animals, because this one you already feel.

You’ve looked a dog in the eye and known someone was home. You’ve watched a cat startle, a crow solve a puzzle, an elephant stand over a dead companion. At some point most of us have felt it: they’re alive the way we’re alive. That’s why cruelty to them makes us flinch.

And yet many traditions draw a hard line. Humans have souls; animals don’t, or have lesser ones. For centuries, serious thinkers argued that animals were machines that only looked as though they felt pain. People still argue about whether fish feel anything.

Look at where that line falls. Biologically, we’re astonishingly close to the animals around us. A chimpanzee shares almost all of our DNA. A dog’s brain has the same basic parts as ours, running on the same chemistry. Nervous systems shade into each other smoothly, from worms to fish to mice to dogs to apes to us, and there’s no step anywhere on that slope where something new switches on. In 2012, a group of prominent neuroscientists signed a public declaration that many animals, including all mammals and birds, have what it takes for conscious experience.

And the soul was never supposed to be about intelligence in the first place. It’s the light that makes something alive and feeling. On that, the dog and you are on the same footing.

So where do you draw the line? You don’t, because there isn’t one. The only place it has ever been drawn is right around us, and we’re the ones who drew it. A line that falls exactly where it flatters the one drawing it is the name in the sand again.

Seeing without knowing

Now go inside a single human head.

Some people with damage to the visual part of the brain go blind in part of their field of view. Ask them and they’ll tell you, honestly, that they see nothing there. But put an object in that blind area and ask them to guess where it is, and they point to it far more often than chance would allow. One such patient, blind by his own account, walked down a corridor cluttered with obstacles and stepped around every one of them. This is called blindsight.

Something in that person saw, and it guided their body. The “I” who talks had no idea. If the soul is the single point where seeing happens, which one is it: the part that saw, or the part that says it didn’t?

Two halves, one story

The clearest case comes from surgery.

For some people with severe epilepsy, surgeons used to cut the thick bundle of fibres that connects the two halves of the brain. The patients recovered well and seemed completely normal. But careful experiments showed that the two halves could no longer share what they knew.

Two facts make the experiment work. Each half of the brain sees one side of what you’re looking at and controls the opposite hand. And in most people, only the left half can talk.

In one famous test, a patient was shown two pictures at once, one to each half. The left half saw a chicken’s foot. The right half saw a snowy scene. Then he was asked to pick, from a row of cards, the ones that went with what he’d seen. His right hand, run by the left half, picked a chicken. His left hand, run by the right half, picked a shovel, for the snow.

Then he was asked why he’d picked the shovel.

The talking half had never seen the snow. It had no idea why the left hand did what it did. And without a moment’s hesitation he said: “Oh, that’s simple. The chicken claw goes with the chicken, and you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed.”

He wasn’t lying, and he wasn’t confused. The talking half had a gap, filled it instantly with a reasonable story, and believed the story. In another version, the silent half was shown the word “walk”, and the patient got up and started walking. Asked where he was going, he said he wanted to get a drink. The scientists who ran these studies gave the storyteller in the left half a name: the interpreter.

It isn’t only split brains

You could say these are damaged brains. But healthy brains do the same thing all the time.

In table tennis, the ball comes at you faster than your eyes can guide a return. Your brain predicts where the ball will be and starts the swing before you’ve consciously seen it arrive. Afterwards, it feels as though you saw the ball and decided to hit it. Kurzgesagt has a good video on this, Why Your Brain Blinds You For 2 Hours Every Day: your body handles a lot of decisions on its own, and you get told about them a little late.

Or take an experiment on what psychologists call choice blindness. People were shown two photos of faces and asked which one they found more attractive. Then the experimenter handed them their chosen photo and asked why they’d picked it. Except sometimes, with a sleight of hand, it was the other photo. Most people didn’t notice. They explained why they’d chosen it, sometimes pointing to features, like earrings, that only the photo they’d rejected had.

Put it together. The “I” that seems to see everything and decide everything is, much of the time, a narrator that arrives after the fact. It explains decisions made elsewhere, using information it doesn’t have, and it believes its own explanations. Psychologists call this post hoc rationalisation, or in its more extreme forms, confabulation. The single point where everything comes together turns out to be a story the brain tells afterwards.

And the hero of that story is always “me”. That’s the ego’s favourite story.

When the soul goes supernatural

In What Is Ego? I covered what ego costs us day to day: fragile pride, conversations turned into contests. When the ego-shaped soul gets taken for something supernatural, the costs get stranger.

Take astrology. Stars many light years away, burning for billions of years, are supposedly arranged to tell one particular monkey whether Tuesday is a good day for a job interview. Numerology says the same about the digits in your birth date. It’s hard to imagine a belief more flattering to the self: the universe is about me.

And it traps spiritual people too, sometimes more than anyone. Someone does the hard work. They meditate for years, step back from money and status, and get free of a big part of the ego. Then they start claiming they can levitate, or read minds, or perform miracles. The ego didn’t go anywhere. It moved, from the body to the soul. “I’m not this body, I’m a soul, and my soul is special.” There’s even a name for this: spiritual materialism, the ego using spiritual practice to build itself back up.

Or look at the usual picture of enlightenment: someone weeping with bliss, lost in ecstasy. There’s nothing wrong with those experiences. I argued in What Is Ego? that some of the most intense highs a person can have come when the sense of self drops away. The trap is chasing them, and treating them as proof you’ve arrived. That’s the same chase you set out to escape, in robes this time.

The flip side is just as common. Some people decide pleasure is dangerous and action binds you, so they back away from both, afraid that enjoying anything will create attachment and attachment will create suffering. Chasing bliss and fearing pleasure look like opposites, but they share a root. Both are completely absorbed in the fate of one particular soul. A lot of the people who’ve worked hardest to escape the ego end up more preoccupied with themselves than anyone. (Fear of attachment deserves its own piece, and I’ll come back to it.)

Why I still believe in a soul

So do I think there’s no soul? No. I don’t believe there’s an object called a soul sitting inside the body, or waiting in some other dimension. But I do believe in a soul, and here’s why.

Reasoning is a tool, and tools serve purposes. In physics and maths, the purpose is matching reality, and those fields are so successful that we’ve come to think matching reality is what reasoning is for. It isn’t always. A lot of good reasoning aims at something else. You don’t tell a grieving friend every true thing you know. A good therapist nudges people with stories before they’re ready for facts. Parents tell children things that aren’t literally true to help them grow. “Reasoning usually aims at truth” became “reasoning is about truth”: a correlation mistaken for a cause. (More on this in Logic Isn’t About Truth.)

The idea of a soul was made by the mind, which was made by evolution. That doesn’t mean we have to fight it, or tear it down and go hard materialist. In The War Between You and Your Mind I described being born on a sailboat: you can’t stop the wind, but you can use it. Evolution pointed our instruments at survival. We can point the same instruments at whatever we’re actually after. For me, that’s peace.

The mind also has a soft spot I find useful. It responds far better to a person than to a principle. Psychologists call the habit of giving human form to things anthropomorphism. Most of us learn our morals from stories, not lists. You can memorise a list of virtues, or you can read the stories of Krishna, get attached to him, feel something, and find the lessons much easier to live by. (I think God is useful in the same way, and I don’t believe in God as the literal creator of the world either. That’s another piece.)

So I don’t throw the soul away. I split it in two:

  • The vitality: the sense that experience is alive, that it matters, that there’s something it’s like to be here. That part is good.
  • The localisation: the soul in this body, mine, one per person. That’s the part the ego feeds on.

I keep the first and drop the second. We’ve already seen that nothing pins experience to the edges of the body. And if the soul isn’t pinned to one body, there’s no reason to think there’s one per body. So I spread it over everything. One soul, not many. This is the old idea of non-duality. Its best-known version, Advaita Vedanta, says the self in you and the reality underneath everything are one and the same, and that the feeling of being a separate soul is exactly the ego’s confusion.

Then why do I only feel this body?

The obvious objection: if my soul is spread across the universe, why do I only experience what happens to this body?

My answer is that the soul, in this sense, doesn’t act, and it doesn’t speak. Everything that reports, everything that says “I see red” or “my hand hurts”, is the body: matter taking in information and putting it back out. And the body can only report on what passed through it.

Think about looking at something red. One part of the story is the body’s: light hits the eye, the brain sorts it, and you can say “red” and tell it apart from green. A machine could do that much. The other part is what the red is like, the redness itself. Philosophers call that quality qualia, and nobody knows how matter produces it. That quality, the living part of experience, is what I call soul. And nothing ties it to this one body. The body just can’t talk about anything else, because the soul never talks at all.

So the body isn’t the living thing with a light inside. It’s an instrument, moving through a world that’s alive everywhere. That’s what ego death feels like from the inside: not losing yourself, but noticing that “yourself” was drawn far too small.

Where this lands

The usual soul, a light inside the body, one per person, mine, special, surviving me, has the ego’s fingerprints all over it. It’s a default we’re born with rather than a conclusion we reached. Its border is the brain’s body map, which a rubber hand can fool. The single point where it supposedly sees and decides keeps turning out to be a narrator telling a story afterwards. And the line it draws between us and the animals falls exactly where it flatters us.

None of that proves there’s no soul. It shows where the odds lean, the same way a name in the sand does.

What I keep is the part worth keeping: the sense that experience is alive and matters, without the fence around one body. I don’t hold that as a fact about the universe. I hold it because it’s useful: it lets me keep the wonder and drop the self-importance.
