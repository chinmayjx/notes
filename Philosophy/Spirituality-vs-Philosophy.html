Spirituality vs Philosophy

Spirituality vs Philosophy

Nov 02, 20265 min read

  • philosophy
  • spirituality

A working line between the two, and the corner cases that test it

I keep using the words philosophy and spirituality side by side, and I wanted a line between them that I can actually work with. Not a perfect one. The edge is fuzzy, and I’ll show where. Just a working line, so that the next time I say “spiritual” I know what I mean.

Philosophy

Philosophy is the inquiry into what’s true, by reasoning.

That used to cover almost everything. Physics was once called natural philosophy. Whenever a question found a way to be checked, by experiment or by proof, it stepped out of philosophy and became a science. What stayed behind are the questions nobody has a way to check yet: what it all means, where we came from, how to live.

Spirituality is philosophy too

Spirituality also has a picture of reality with claims in it: there’s a soul, there’s karma, the world is an illusion, there’s God. It answers the same leftover questions. So I count it as a part of philosophy, not something beside it.

What sets it apart is where it starts. Every way of thinking rests on a few starting assumptions it doesn’t prove; logicians call them axioms (more on that in What Is Logic?). Spirituality’s starting assumptions include something beyond matter: a soul, God, karma, rebirth. It usually takes them on trust, from a scripture, a teacher or someone’s experience, rather than arguing for them. But everything built on top of them can be reasoned about like any other philosophy. That’s why it’s a part of philosophy and not a different kind of thing.

The second trait is that spiritual frameworks are aimed at the self: peace, liberation, union with God, an end to suffering. They’re built to change how you feel and live, not only what you think is true.

So here’s the definition I’m going with:

Spirituality is philosophy aimed at the self’s peace or liberation, built on something beyond matter that concerns the self: a soul, God, karma or rebirth.

I first thought the beyond-matter part was enough on its own. The corner cases showed it takes both.

The corner cases

Put the two traits on a grid and most cases fall into place.

Buddhism has no soul. It explicitly denies a lasting self. It’s still clearly spiritual: rebirth, karma and nirvana are all beyond matter, and the whole thing is aimed at ending suffering. That’s why the definition says “something beyond matter” and not “a soul”.

Stoicism aims at peace but needs nothing beyond matter, at least the way people practise it today, and the same goes for secular mindfulness. Both have practices, like reflecting on your day or sitting with your breath, and both are after a calm mind. By this definition they’re philosophy with practices, not spirituality, and I think that’s right. Seeking peace doesn’t make something spiritual.

A non-material mind, or a proof that God exists. Philosophers have argued for both. They’re beyond matter, but they’re arguments about what exists, not a path for the self to walk. That’s metaphysics. Mathematicians who believe numbers really exist outside the physical world are in the same box, and nobody calls them spiritual. So being beyond matter doesn’t make something spiritual either.

Superstition. Lucky numbers, the evil eye. Beyond matter, but aimed at luck rather than the self’s peace. Not spiritual.

“Spiritual but not religious.” Some people call the awe they feel at the night sky, or feeling connected to nature, spiritual, with nothing beyond matter in it. Everyday speech uses the word more widely than my definition does, and that’s fine. I’m drawing a working line, not policing the word.

Practices

Spirituality comes with practices, and they range widely. At one end is meditation, which anyone can try and which works without believing anything. In the middle is devotion like bhakti: you may not share its assumptions, but it makes sense as a practice and does what it’s meant to do. At the far end is the occult.

The practices aren’t what defines it, though. The Stoics had practices too.

Starting points, not conclusions

The definition is about what a framework starts from, not what it concludes. That matters, because plenty of ideas people think of as spiritual aren’t.

Take “attachment causes suffering”. The Buddha taught it, but you don’t need a soul or rebirth to get there. You can get there by watching your own mind: you cling to something, you fear losing it, and you suffer when you do. People who believe in nothing beyond matter hold it too.

So when an idea like that goes wrong in someone’s life, spirituality isn’t the culprit as such. The idea is, whichever framework happens to carry it. That’s a story for another piece.
