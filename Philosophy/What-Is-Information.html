What Is Information?

What Is Information?

Oct 18, 202610 min read

  • philosophy
  • information

Start with the obvious answer, find what’s wrong with it, and keep going

Here’s a question that sounds easy: what is information?

We use the word all the time. We look information up, we worry about having too much of it, we say we live in the information age. But try to say what it actually is and it slips away. For some reason this topic is very hard for the brain to hold on to. Every time I think I’ve got a definition, something about it feels off.

In Logic Isn’t About Truth I described logic as a way of moving through information. This piece is about the thing underneath: the information itself. I’ll start with the obvious answer, find what’s wrong with it, fix it, and repeat. Where I end up is stranger than where I started, but I think it’s closer to the truth, and the way there is the interesting part.

First try: a picture in your head

Start with where the idea of information lives: in my head. And when I look at what else is in my head, I find a rough copy of the world.

Get up at night and walk to the kitchen without turning on the light. You don’t walk into the door frame, because you aren’t relying on your eyes. You’re walking through a copy of your house that you carry around. You know where the counter starts and where the step is, and the copy is good enough that your body trusts it.

That’s what makes the copy useful. I can move things around inside it and find out what would happen outside, without trying it for real. So here’s the face-value answer: information is a picture in a mind that lets it predict the world.

It fits most of what we call information. What you know about your friends, your city, your job: all of it is a copy you check before you act.

Second try: it doesn’t need a mind

Here’s the problem. By that definition, information can’t exist unless a mind is holding it.

Picture a notebook written by someone who died a hundred years ago. Nobody alive has what’s in it in their head. Is it information? Obviously. Open it and it tells you about the world. A map is the same: it still shows where the road bends, long after the person who drew it is gone.

So the mind isn’t the important part. What matters is that the map has something in common with the land. The map is a small pattern, ink on paper, with the same layout as a big pattern, roads on the ground. Because their shapes match, reading one tells you about the other.

Second try, then: information is a pattern, anywhere, whose shape matches another pattern well enough that you can learn about one from the other.

Notice what happened to the line the first try drew between the mind and the world. It’s gone. The notebook isn’t outside reality looking in. It’s a stack of paper in a drawer, part of the world like everything else. So is your brain. Information isn’t something that stands apart from reality and describes it. It’s one part of reality that resembles another part.

Third try: it doesn’t need a reader

There’s still someone hiding in that definition: me. “You can learn about one from the other.” Who’s doing the learning? If nobody ever opens the notebook, does it stop being information?

Think about a tree. Every year it adds a ring, wide in good years and narrow in dry ones. For three hundred years nobody looks. Then the tree comes down, someone counts the rings, and reads off three centuries of rainfall.

Did counting the rings put the information there? No. The rings matched the weather the whole time. The person who counted them only noticed something that was already true.

That’s how you take yourself out of the definition. Calling something information doesn’t change it. It only says how I’m using it, the way a stone becomes a “paperweight” when I put it on my papers. Take me away, and the pattern is still there, still matching whatever made it. If my label is what makes it information, it was information before I arrived.

And once you look, almost every pattern matches something. A footprint matches a foot. A smooth pebble matches the river that rolled it. Starlight carries the makeup of the star that sent it. Things leave traces of themselves in each other; that’s what it means for them to touch.

So, third try: every pattern is information. The universe, the biggest pattern there is, is information all the way through.

Isn’t that circular?

Now there’s a problem. If every pattern is information, what’s a pattern? An arrangement of smaller patterns. In other words, information. I’m using the word to define itself.

Usually that’s the sign of a bad definition. “A dog is a dog” tells you nothing. But not every circular definition is empty.

Here’s one you use every day: a folder is something that holds files and other folders. The word “folder” appears in its own definition, and you still understand folders completely. The definition even tells you the most interesting thing about them: they can sit inside each other as deep as you like. Programmers write functions like this all the time, functions that call themselves. The definition calls itself, and it’s still a complete description of what the function will do.

The difference is that “a dog is a dog” only repeats a word. The folder definition describes how the thing behaves, and the circle is part of the behaviour.

I think information is like that. If it really is the most basic thing there is, there’s nothing more basic to define it with. All you can do is describe how it behaves. (Geometry does the same with points: it never says what a point is, only how points and lines behave, and builds everything from there.) Here’s how information behaves: it’s made of patterns, patterns are made of smaller patterns, and some patterns resemble others. When one resembles another, it tells you something about it.

Where does motion live?

Next problem. Patterns sound still: shapes, arrangements, layouts. But the world moves. Throw a ball and it rises and falls. Where is the information that it’s going to fall?

It isn’t in any single moment. Take a sharp photo of a ball in mid-air and you can’t tell whether it’s on its way up or on its way down. One slice of the world doesn’t hold its motion.

You already know where motion goes, though. Put time along the bottom of a graph and motion turns into a shape. A stock chart is a year of rises and drops drawn as one still line. Plot the ball’s height against time and its whole flight becomes an arc, sitting on the page, complete.

Physicists take this literally. In Einstein’s relativity, time is a dimension alongside the three of space, and one common way to read it is that the universe is a single four-dimensional shape that never changes. Physicists call this the block universe. The ball’s flight is part of that shape, the same way a slope is part of a mountain. Nothing in the block moves. Motion is what the shape looks like from inside, one slice at a time, like a flipbook: every page is still, and the movement is in the stack.

That also answers where the “must” in “the ball must fall” comes from. A law of physics is a regularity in the shape: every thrown ball, everywhere, traces the same kind of arc.

So once time is part of the picture, the problem goes away. Everything really is information: one enormous pattern, stretched across space and time.

Putting ourselves back in

Now I can go back to the first try, the picture in your head, and see what it was. It wasn’t wrong. It was a special case.

We don’t get to see the shape all at once. We live one slice at a time, always in the present, with no view along time. If you want to know what comes next, the only way is to build a small copy of the pattern that runs faster than the real thing.

A weather forecast does exactly this. A computer runs next week’s weather in a few hours. The copy has the same shape as the real weather, but it lives on a different substrate (chips instead of air) and it’s squeezed into a much shorter time. Because it gets to the end first, it tells you what’s coming. Your head does the same thing when you picture the drive to the airport in a few seconds and decide to leave at six.

So the information we care about, the kind the first try called “a picture in a mind”, is this: a small pattern on one substrate, running over a short time, with the same shape as a big pattern running over a long one. The copy finishes before the world does. That’s all a prediction is.

Patterns on patterns

One loose end. I said the copy lives on a different substrate. But a brain is part of the physical world, and so is a computer. Isn’t it all one substrate in the end?

It is, and this is where the circle comes back. A substrate is just a pattern that other patterns can be built on. Think of a video game. Mario runs, jumps and bumps into blocks, and his world has its own rules. You reason about it entirely in those rules. Underneath, it’s code; under the code, a chip; under the chip, electrons. Each layer is a pattern in the layer below, and the ground for the layer above.

Minds work the same way. Ideas sit on neurons, neurons on chemistry, chemistry on physics. We call the top layer the mind, or the world of ideas, because that’s the layer we can reason in. Nobody thinks in neurons. Substrates nest inside each other, just like folders.

Where this lands

Here’s where I’ve ended up. Everything is information: patterns made of smaller patterns, sitting on substrates that are patterns themselves. Some patterns resemble others. When a small, fast pattern resembles a big, slow one, it can tell you how the big one ends. That’s the kind of information living things learned to build, because we’re stuck in the present and have no other way to see what’s coming.

I started with information as something inside me, and ended with me as something inside information. Which raises the next question: if a mind is a small, fast copy of the world, what is the “I” that seems to be doing the copying? That one gets its own article.
