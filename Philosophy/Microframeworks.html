Microframeworks

Microframeworks

Oct 21, 202614 min read

  • philosophy
  • microframeworks

There’s no single “you” in charge, and peace doesn’t have to wait

Background

Some of the groundwork is in separate pieces, linked along the way. You don’t need to read them first.

Think about the thing you’re working towards right now: a job, an exam, a house, a person. There’s probably a sentence that comes with it. Once this is done, I can relax.

This whole philosophy comes from taking one question about that sentence seriously:

If you’ll be at peace once you’ve done this thing, why can’t you be at peace first, and then do it?

It sounds like a trick question. I don’t think it is. To answer it, I had to work out what we’re actually chasing, and then take apart the idea that there’s a single “you” doing the chasing.

What we’re chasing: peace, not happiness

The obvious answer is happiness. I think that answer is a correlation mistaken for a cause.

Most of the time, happiness and peace arrive together. You get the job, you feel happy, and for a while you stop wanting anything to change. Pain usually arrives with struggle: you want it gone. Because the pairs show up together so reliably, we assume happiness is what produces peace. Get happy, and peace will follow. So we chase happiness.

But look at the moments where the two come apart. We watch sad films. We eat food so spicy it hurts, ride roller coasters, and stand in a stadium screaming, heart pounding, in something close to rage. All of these feel bad by any emotional measure. And yet, in the middle of them, we don’t want them to stop.

That last part is the test. It isn’t pain now for a reward later, like an ice bath you endure for the glow afterwards. It’s discomfort while not wanting things to be any different. That is what I mean by peace: not struggling against whatever you’re experiencing.

If peace can show up alongside discomfort, then happiness isn’t what produces it. The two usually travel together, and that’s all. Peace isn’t a particular feeling; it’s a relationship to whatever you’re feeling, and sadness can have it as easily as joy. So if what we’re really after is the peace, then chasing happiness means chasing the thing that usually comes along with it, and hoping.

I don’t need to claim that everyone secretly wants peace. A weaker claim is enough. Plenty of people look honestly at their lives and notice the loop: want something, get it, settle for a while, want the next thing. (I’ve written about that loop, the hedonic treadmill, in Why the Age of Abundance Won’t Feel Like One.) If that loop doesn’t satisfy you, peace is something you can aim at directly, instead of hoping it arrives at the end of a list.

The usual picture: you versus your mind

Ask how people try to get there and you’ll usually hear some version of one picture. There’s you, the part that plans and sees far, and there’s your mind, the cravings and moods that keep dragging you off course. Discipline is you winning; failure is your mind winning. I wrote about that picture in The War Between You and Your Mind, and for everyday purposes I think it’s a good one.

For a long time, it was my whole philosophy. Then, taken all the way, it started producing two problems.

The first was frustration that never ends. Why can’t I just take control? Why do I need tricks? Worse, it poisoned good experiences after the fact. I’d work passionately for hours, and afterwards a voice would say: that wasn’t really you, that was ambition. Every experience became an interrogation of motives.

The second was paralysis. Once I decided most worldly goals were driven by “the mind”, my motivation for ordinary goals drained away. But the cravings I’d written off didn’t stop. They kept running my days, just without my approval.

A philosophy meant to bring peace had become a reliable source of guilt. So I went back and looked more carefully at the “I” at the centre of it.

There’s no single you

Take a sentence like “I didn’t want to do it, but my mind made me.” Afterwards we say “that wasn’t me.” Who is the “I” in each half of that sentence?

Physically, there’s no separate self and no separate mind. There’s one system, a brain and body reacting to the world. “Self” and “mind” are labels we draw over its behaviour, and the line between them moves. Whatever we disapprove of afterwards gets moved to the other side.

There’s a striking piece of evidence for how loosely the “I” sits on top. In some patients whose brain halves have been surgically separated, you can show an instruction to only the half that can’t speak, and the patient carries it out. Ask the speaking half why they did it, and it doesn’t say “I don’t know.” It invents a confident, reasonable-sounding explanation on the spot. The part that narrates isn’t the part that decides; it’s the part that explains.

Microframeworks

So if there’s no single self, what is the “I” that wants things?

Watch it across one day. In the morning, I want to work hard. By afternoon I want a break. In the evening I want company, and at midnight I want one more video. Each of these comes with its own priorities, its own sense of what matters, and its own idea of who “I” am. They don’t feel like moods of one fixed person. They feel like different people taking turns to say “I”.

I call each of these a microframework: a small, temporary set of assumptions, plus whatever information happens to be in view, plus its own meaning for the word “I”. “Framework” is meant in the logician’s sense, as a set of starting assumptions and everything that follows from them (see What Is Logic). The “micro” is because they’re small and short-lived. What really separates one from another is what it takes for granted. “I need a break” and “I want to work hard” aren’t arguing from the same starting point, which is why they never convince each other.

That also settles what counts as one framework: a framework is defined by its axioms, its starting assumptions. You rarely think about them while you’re inside it, but every bit of reasoning you do starts from some, so it always belongs to some framework. In the moment, you can’t see the edges. Looking back, you always can. The moment “I need a break” took over from “I want to work hard” is the moment the starting assumptions changed. And they’re micro because they don’t last: a few minutes, an hour, an evening.

Two images help me hold this.

A spotlight. Picture all the ways you could think about your life as a huge dark stage, and a spotlight that lights up one small part at a time. You don’t choose where it goes. Your surroundings, your memories, how much you slept, and what you scrolled past all move it. Even “choosing” happens inside whichever part is lit at the time.

Peripheral vision. Your eyes only see sharply in a small area; the rest is blur. Inside a framework it’s the same. You reason sharply about a few things, while most of your assumptions sit blurry at the edges, never examined.

What changes when you see it this way

You plan for your future changes of mind. If the “I” making a plan isn’t a permanent manager, plan for the one who’ll be in charge later. Deleting Instagram from your phone isn’t an admission of weakness. It’s sensible preparation by a framework that knows a different one will be running at eleven tonight.

Regret loses its sting. The “I” judging yesterday’s mistake has different priorities from the “I” that made it. “I had no self-control” is one framework’s opinion of another, with no special authority. You didn’t fail at being yourself. You were a different you.

But it isn’t a free pass. All these frameworks share one body, and what one does shapes the next. One framework finds peace in a whole pizza at midnight, and the morning framework wakes up sluggish and anxious. That’s what I mean by karma: not cosmic bookkeeping, just consequences passed from one framework to the next through the body they share. Discipline still matters. It just works by shaping conditions, not by an inner commander winning a fight.

Things matter, locally

Where do our purposes come from? Upbringing, culture, personal history, and the survival instincts evolution put in us. Seeing that doesn’t make a purpose false. You can know exactly why you love your family and love them just as much.

But it does change how much authority a purpose gets. Here’s my position, as precisely as I can put it: everything that matters, matters inside some framework. Brushing your teeth matters a lot to the framework doing it, and very little to the one planning your year. Your country matters intensely to the patriotic one. None of that is fake. What I deny is that any purpose matters outside every framework, written into the universe and binding on everyone. Things matter locally. Nothing is commanded absolutely. (Why I’m comfortable judging beliefs by usefulness instead of final truth is in Logic Isn’t About Truth.)

This is what I mean by creating distance from a purpose. You don’t stop caring. You see the scope of the care: where it came from, which frameworks it lives in, and the fact that your peace doesn’t have to wait for it.

You can take that distance at different depths:

  • Keep the purpose fully, and see whether caring a little less about the outcome makes you steadier, and better at it.
  • Look at where it came from, and loosen how much of your identity rests on it.
  • Make peace itself your main aim, and keep the other purposes as commitments you choose.

Picture a soldier who keeps serving their country while knowing their patriotism was taught, not discovered. They still serve. They’re just not at war with every setback.

The orchestrator

Something in you is reading this and thinking about how to live better. I call it the orchestrator: the frameworks that understand all of this and try to coordinate the rest.

It isn’t a manager outside the system; that would just bring back the single self. It runs on the same brain as everything else, and it comes and goes like any other framework. Tonight the spotlight will move and the orchestrator will go dark. That’s expected.

The difference from the old picture is one sentence: the old self believed its “I”. The orchestrator uses its “I”. The war model took “me” as a fact. There really was a war, and a self who ought to win, so every loss was a real failure and guilt was built in. The orchestrator treats “I” as a working assumption, the way a mathematician treats the square root of minus one: it doesn’t exist as an ordinary number, and it’s enormously useful anyway. When another framework takes over, “that was a different I” is already part of the plan. There’s nothing to feel guilty about.

Its job is coordination, not control. Think of the frameworks as employees: the ambitious one, the caring one, the curious one, the one who wants a nap. A good company doesn’t need them all to share one personality. It needs them pulling in roughly the same direction instead of sabotaging each other. The orchestrator is the manager, and it works mostly through conditions: what you learn, what you surround yourself with, which triggers you remove, and which good habits you make easy.

And its wanting is different. The orchestrator wants the rest of the system to be at peace, and wanting is still wanting. But it’s wanting the way you want to win a game you’re enjoying: full engagement, nothing missing. Not wanting from lack, but wanting as play.

Living it: distance without losing the drive

There’s an obvious cost here, and I won’t pretend otherwise. Distance works against motivation. If you decide nothing matters absolutely and then try to act, you won’t act with the hunger of someone who’s certain the next promotion is everything. A greedy person with no doubts will outwork a detached one.

The way out isn’t to pick one framework and force the rest to agree with it. That’s the cognitive dissonance that made the war model so exhausting. The way out is to let all of them in. Hindu thought has a word for this: lila, play. Krishna knows the whole cosmic drama is a drama, and plays every role in it with complete passion anyway.

That’s how the orchestrator relates to the frameworks. Each employee takes its own goal completely seriously, and it should, because that’s where the drive comes from. The ambitious one really wants the promotion. The caring one really wants the family to be all right. The orchestrator doesn’t need to share their seriousness. It decides who’s on shift today, and keeps their goals lined up with the company’s. The detachment sits with the manager; the drive stays with the employees. That’s how you get distance and motivation together.

In practice, this is the old Indian idea of karma yoga: work fully towards your goals, and hold the outcomes lightly.

Some practical consequences:

  • Let every motive help. Ambition, curiosity, affection and enjoyment are all fuel. The goal isn’t to suppress them; it’s to point them in compatible directions.
  • Prepare for the spotlight to move. Change your surroundings, remove predictable triggers, make good responses easy. That’s engineering, not weakness.
  • Treat frustration as a signal. When you catch yourself condemning a past action, look at the conditions that produced it, and change those.
  • Expect to forget all this. You’ll get absorbed, lose the outlook, and get lost in the chase again. That doesn’t disprove it. The outlook spreads slowly across many frameworks, never all at once.

This also explains why nobody gets perfectly disciplined, however much they practise. Practice changes where the spotlight tends to land. It never pins it in place, because nobody is holding it. Push hard enough (real pain, real fear) and it gets dragged regardless. You can shift the odds; you can’t freeze them.

It even explains what reading something like this does. It isn’t instructions to some boss standing outside the system, because there’s no such boss. It’s conditioning. It makes frameworks like the orchestrator light up a little more often.

My choice: optimistic nihilism

So where does this leave purpose? Plainly: I’m an optimistic nihilist. There’s no inherent purpose, nothing that has to be done. Whatever goal I hold is a choice, and an arbitrary one.

That includes the bigger ideas. A universal self, or a soul everyone shares, is a useful concept that frameworks inside this body came up with, not a fact I discovered. I’ll write about that separately.

Since the choice is arbitrary anyway, I made one: not to be steered by ego, the bias that puts this one body at the centre of everything. So I define my goal, loosely, as the well-being of people in general. And I’m not very serious about it. The roles I play might be: the engineer about the product, the friend about their friends, the citizen about the country. Each of them pursues its own goal, sometimes fiercely. The orchestrator only does the manager’s job, keeping the employees’ goals pointed roughly where the company is going.

That’s not a doctrine to defend. It’s a choice I made, and you’re free to make a different one.

So, back to the question: if you’ll be at peace once you’ve done this thing, why can’t you be at peace first, and then do it?

My answer is that you can. Not by winning the war with your mind, but by noticing that the one who’d win it was never a single person. Do the thing, fully. Just don’t make your peace wait for it.
