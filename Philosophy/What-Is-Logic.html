What Is Logic?

What Is Logic?

Oct 20, 20269 min read

  • philosophy
  • logic

Language, premises, rules, frameworks, and why one contradiction ruins everything

In What Is Information I ended up somewhere simple. Everything is information, and the kind we use is a small, fast pattern that has the same shape as a big, slow one. A weather forecast runs next week in a few hours. Because the copy finishes first, it tells you what’s coming.

With that in place, logic turns out to be much easier to define. If information is the copy, logic is how we run it.

The copy needs somewhere to live

A copy has to be written on something. For most of our thinking, that something is language.

Language does two jobs. It names things: the glass, the table, the floor. And it stores claims about them, of two kinds:

  • How things are: “the glass is on the edge of the table.”
  • What follows what: “a glass that falls off a table breaks.”

Put the two together and you can run the world forward without touching it. The glass is at the edge; things at the edge get knocked off; glasses that fall break. Move the glass. Nothing happened in the world. A few words moved around in your head, and they told you how the next ten seconds could go.

Language folds time

Here’s what makes language so good at this. It squeezes time.

Take “it rains every second day.” In the world, that fact is enormous. It’s spread across every day there is, maybe forever. Language turns the repetition itself into a thing, gives it a name, and stores it in one sentence you can carry around.

It folds time in a second way too. A line of reasoning keeps its own history. Write out a sum, or think something through, and the earlier steps are still there when you reach the end. Where you started and where you ended up, the present and the future you worked out from it, sit on the same page.

Pictures can do some of this. Plenty of people think visually, and most animals probably think only in pictures. But pictures run out quickly. Try picturing “every second day, forever.” You can say it easily. You can’t really see it.

Premises and axioms: where the copy starts

Every piece of reasoning starts somewhere. In an everyday argument, the starting claims are called premises. “The glass is at the edge” and “glasses that fall break” are premises. When the starting claims define a whole system, like geometry, arithmetic or a game, they’re called axioms. Either way, they do the same job: they’re statements you don’t prove, you just take.

Axioms describe the system. They introduce the parts (the nouns) and how the parts behave (the verbs).

Chess is the cleanest example. The axioms are the rules of the game. There are pieces, a king, a bishop, a knight, and each one moves in a certain way. Nobody proves that a bishop moves diagonally. It’s simply how this system is defined.

Rules: how the copy is allowed to move

You already know how to do the next part. Nobody needs a course to see that “every glass breaks when dropped; this is a glass; so it will break if dropped” works, or that “some glasses are blue; this is a glass; so it’s blue” doesn’t. We all notice when a step doesn’t follow. Formal logic is an attempt to write that instinct down precisely.

The steps are governed by rules of inference. The most famous one (logicians call it modus ponens): if A, and if A then B, then B. The bishop is on this square; if a bishop is on this square, it can reach that one; so it can reach that one.

When the conclusion of an argument follows from its premises like this, the argument is valid. A good rule never takes you from true premises to a false conclusion, whatever they’re about. That’s what logicians actually mean by logic: the study of which steps are safe by their shape alone. “Every dropped glass breaks; this is a glass; so it breaks if dropped” has exactly the same shape as “every cat can fly; Tom is a cat; so Tom can fly”. Both are valid. Whether the conclusion is true depends entirely on the premises. An argument that is valid and starts from true premises is called sound. The flying cat is valid, but not sound.

The rules are a choice too. The standard set is called classical logic, but there are others. Some refuse certain steps the standard set allows, and some are built to tolerate contradictions. Most logicians treat the rules as pure form, known without looking at the world. My own hunch is that the rules we find natural were learned from the world as well: we trust “if A then B” because, over and over, things that followed each other kept following. That’s my view, not the textbook one.

A framework is everything the axioms reach

Start from the axioms and keep applying the rules, and you get a growing tree of consequences: everything that follows. (In mathematics, a consequence someone has actually proved is called a theorem.) Logicians call the whole tree a theory. In everyday English “theory” sounds like a hunch, so I’ll call it a framework, a word philosophers have used in much the same sense.

The odd thing about a framework is that it’s fixed the moment you choose its axioms and rules. Every game of chess that could ever be played is already in the rules. Nobody has played them all, and nobody will. Reasoning doesn’t add anything to a framework; it only uncovers more of what was already there.

Fixed doesn’t mean it answers everything, though. Some frameworks contain questions their own axioms can’t settle either way. Gödel proved that ordinary arithmetic is one of them, which shook mathematics when it came out.

So the question isn’t really when reasoning runs out. It’s how far you can see. A grandmaster and a beginner play inside exactly the same framework. The grandmaster sees ten moves deep, and the beginner sees two.

Why one contradiction ruins everything

So logic isn’t the framework itself. Logic is the rules for growing it: the steps that never lead from truth to falsehood.

The one thing those rules can’t survive is a contradiction: a claim and its opposite, a copy pointing two ways at once. In standard logic, the damage doesn’t stay local. Once a framework contains a contradiction, you can derive anything from it: the glass breaks, the glass doesn’t break, the moon is made of cheese. Logicians call this explosion. A framework that says everything predicts nothing.

That’s why there’s really only one hard requirement for a set of axioms: they must be consistent, meaning no contradiction can be derived from them. Logicians also like axioms to be independent, meaning none of them can be derived from the others. That keeps things tidy, but nothing breaks if one is redundant.

To falsify a claim is to find it going the other way: it says the glass falls, and the glass doesn’t fall. Close to the ground, that’s easy to see. As the claims get more abstract, you can’t picture them anymore, and that’s exactly where the rules earn their keep. They let you go where your imagination can’t follow. That’s the beauty of abstraction: you can reason your way through systems that feel completely counterintuitive and still land somewhere that predicts.

In everyday thinking, you’re the checker

Here’s the part that’s easy to miss. Ordinary language doesn’t stop you contradicting yourself.

Paper will happily hold “the glass broke” two lines above “the glass didn’t break”. Language stores claims and remembers them. It doesn’t check them. In everyday reasoning, the checking is done by whoever is doing the reasoning: you, a teacher marking your working, or a friend who says “wait, you just said the opposite”.

That’s the whole reason logicians formalised logic. Write the axioms and rules precisely enough, and checking becomes mechanical: a computer can verify a proof line by line without understanding any of it. Everyday thinking never gets that luxury. It runs on loose language and the same instinct that spots a bad step, enforced as best we can, which is why it goes wrong. Usually the rules aren’t the problem. The person checking missed something: a premise stated forty lines back, a step that didn’t quite follow.

Putting it together

Here’s the whole thing in one place, in the words logicians use:

  • Information is a small, fast pattern with the same shape as a big, slow one.
  • Language is what we write that pattern on. It names things, stores claims about how they are and what follows what, and folds time into a sentence.
  • Premises (in an argument) and axioms (in a system) are the starting claims, taken without proof.
  • Rules of inference are the steps you’re allowed to take, chosen so they never lead from true premises to a false conclusion.
  • An argument is valid if its conclusion follows from its premises, and sound if it’s valid and its premises are true.
  • A framework is everything the axioms and rules reach. Logicians call it a theory.
  • A framework is consistent if no contradiction can be derived in it. One contradiction, and it explodes.
  • Logic is the study and use of the rules: extending a framework without contradiction, so the copy keeps predicting.

Notice what isn’t in that list: truth. Logic guarantees that each step is safe. Nothing in it checks the premises against the world, and that has consequences, some of them expensive. That’s the subject of Logic Isn’t About Truth.
