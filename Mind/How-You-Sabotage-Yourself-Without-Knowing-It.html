How You Sabotage Yourself Without Knowing It

How You Sabotage Yourself Without Knowing It

Oct 21, 20269 min read

  • mind
  • psychology

Three traps the mind sets for you, why they’re so hard to see, and how to step out of them

Self-sabotage comes in two kinds. In the first, you know exactly what you’re doing. You know the third drink is a bad idea and you have it anyway. That’s a problem of self-control, and it isn’t what this piece is about.

The second kind is harder, because you don’t know you’re doing it. You’re stuck, and the story you tell yourself about why sounds completely reasonable. This piece is about that kind.

In The War Between You and Your Mind I described how evolution built a mind whose priorities aren’t yours. Here I want to look at a few specific places where those priorities set traps: loops that keep a person suffering, and that feel, from the inside, like simply who they are.

What this article is not saying

Depression, anxiety and insomnia are real. Biology may well be the main driver of them: genes, brain chemistry, physical health. They deserve real treatment.

This article is about another factor that sits on top: psychological traps. These can affect anyone, including people with no biological predisposition at all, and they can make a real condition harder to escape.

It’s never one cause. The question isn’t whether a trap is involved (some version of it is involved for everyone) but how much of what keeps you stuck is the trap.

The mind can make you sad, if sadness pays

Start with the part that sounds wrong. Why would your own mind make you sad?

Because sadness can be rewarded. You’ve probably felt this. You’re upset and alone, and you’re holding it together. Then someone sits down next to you and asks if you’re okay, and suddenly it gets worse. Your eyes fill up, and you’re crying in a way you weren’t a minute ago.

Nobody plans that, and nobody’s faking. It’s an old, evolved response: for a social animal, showing distress to someone who cares gets you help. But it shows that the mind can produce sadness when sadness earns something. Psychologists have a name for that payoff: secondary gain, the attention, care or relief a symptom brings with it.

And anything that pays can become a loop. Once a state earns a reward, the mind keeps steering you back into it. Over time it starts to look a lot like addiction: a pleasure that’s bad for you, and hard to put down.

Trap one: the tortured genius

Everyone wants to feel special, and almost everyone wants to feel smart.

There’s a hard way to get that: make something that actually helps people, and let the value show. And there’s an easier way: become the tortured genius. Be visibly troubled. Talk like someone who sees through everything.

You’ve seen the posts. “The curse of knowing too much.” “Ignorance is bliss.” “Happy people just don’t think enough.” “Too smart to be happy.” The message is the same every time: my suffering is proof of my depth.

That message pays. It earns validation from other people, and from yourself. And it strokes the ego from an unexpected direction: I’m sad because I understand. Look at all those dumb, happy people.

This doesn’t mean everyone who talks like this is a fraud. It isn’t black and white. Someone in this trap may really be sharp, and that sensitivity may be exactly what made them prone to it. But being genuine doesn’t make you immune to the mechanism, any more than being a monk stops your brain reacting to weed. The question isn’t whether the person is real. It’s how much of what keeps them stuck is the reward.

(And it isn’t a claim about whether intelligence and mental illness are linked. Maybe they are. That’s a different question.)

Trap two: trauma as a badge

The worst thing about trauma isn’t always the trauma itself. It’s what the trauma becomes afterwards: a ready-made excuse that later traps can lean on. The mind is lazy, and it will use whatever leverage it can find.

I’m not writing this from outside. I grew up in my uncle’s house while my mother lived with schizophrenia, and I lost her slowly, over months. I came out of it with social anxiety, trust issues and trouble sleeping, and every one of them offered me a badge to wear.

Anyone who’s been through something hard reaches a fork. You can fight what it did to you, or you can wear it.

Take someone who grew up feeling like a burden in the house they lived in. They learn to make themselves invisible, and by adulthood it has become social anxiety: every room feels like a place where they’ll be noticed and not wanted. They can work at it, slowly and painfully. Or they can say “I’m like this because of how I grew up”, and stop there.

Once you start looking, the badges are everywhere. “I have daddy issues.” “I grew up in an abusive household, so of course I do drugs.” “My parents divorced, so I don’t do relationships.” “I have trust issues.” “Nobody really wants to talk to me anyway.”

Here’s the uncomfortable part. A healed, ordinary life doesn’t come with a story. If you get better, there’s nothing to explain. If you keep sabotaging your relationships, you get to say “I come from a toxic childhood, so I can’t get close to people”, and that one sentence carries an identity and sympathy at the same time. Nobody decides this consciously. The mind just never steers you towards getting better, because staying where you are keeps paying.

Labels stack traps

The most dangerous labels are the ones that pay in several ways at once.

Say your mother had insomnia, and you have trouble sleeping too. Some of that may well be real. Now it’s two in the morning and your mind wants the phone. “I have insomnia, like my mother” is the perfect excuse. And look at how much that one label feeds.

The excuse to keep scrolling. The sympathy when you mention it. The trauma story. Even the tortured-genius identity, the one who can’t switch off. One label, four payoffs. That’s why some labels are so hard to put down: you’d be giving up four rewards at once.

Trap three: buying certainty

This last trap is quieter, and it doesn’t need anyone else to be watching.

If you’ve played badminton or table tennis, you may have noticed something odd: people often start falling apart just when they’re winning. A run of easy points, then suddenly a string of unforced errors. Here’s what I think is going on. A close, winning game is intense. You have to focus, you’re anxious, and you have to live with not knowing how it ends. Then you throw a few points, and the uncertainty is gone. You’re losing. That’s bad, but at least it’s settled.

The same thing happens off the court. You’re waiting on a result that comes out tomorrow, you have a chance to find out today, and you take it, even when knowing early only makes things worse. The answer isn’t the point. The point is to stop not knowing.

Ideas get the same treatment. You get an idea that could turn into something good, and almost immediately your mind finds a reason to drop it. It’s been done. It won’t work. You don’t have time. Sometimes those reasons are right. But often what’s really being avoided is the months of hard, uncertain work the idea would ask for. “It probably wouldn’t have worked anyway” ends the uncertainty tonight.

The visible version of this is familiar: you know the answer in class and still can’t raise your hand. You know you’re scared, so that one isn’t hidden. The hidden versions are the ones above, where the fear dresses up as a sensible reason.

The pattern underneath

All three traps share one root. The mind is an algorithm shaped by evolution, and it was never shaped for your well-being. It reaches for whatever pays right now: validation, sympathy, relief from uncertainty. It doesn’t check whether the payment ruins your next ten years.

And these traps are rarely all or nothing. Every behaviour has many causes. Your biology, your history and your circumstances all pull at once, and the trap is one more force on top of them. But it’s the one you can do something about most directly.

The way out

The answer is the same in every case: don’t take your thoughts and feelings at face value. Look at the motive behind them.

Name the payoff. When you catch yourself saying “I’m like this because…”, ask what that sentence gets you. Sympathy? A story? Permission not to try? Relief from not knowing? You don’t have to say it out loud. You just have to see it.

Follow the argument one more step. “I’m like this because of X, so I can’t” is a shallow argument. It stops exactly where it’s convenient. Ask: can’t, or haven’t yet? Does everyone with X end up like this? (In Heart or Mind Is the Wrong Question I argued that the real question is always how deep an argument goes.) Most of these fall apart at the second step.

Get the reward the hard way. The needs underneath are real: to feel special, to be cared for, to be close to people, to feel settled. The trap is only a cheap route to them. Make something and let it speak for you. Build closeness instead of being pitied into it. Practise sitting with uncertainty, a little longer each time.

Be patient with yourself. The loop has been paying you for years. It won’t vanish the moment you notice it, and the tools from The War Between You and Your Mind (meditation, patience, being soft on yourself) all apply here. But noticing is where it starts. A trap you can see is already half open.
