The Fairy Tale and the Animal

The Fairy Tale and the Animal

Nov 05, 202611 min read

  • mind
  • relationships
  • evolution

Facing the dark side we all carry, and keeping the good side alive once we have

Everyone has a dark side. Most of us spend our lives quietly looking away from it. So why look?

Because it’s right there, and looking away doesn’t make it go anywhere. Sooner or later it finds you. A friend drifts off the month you stop being useful to them. You catch yourself enjoying a rival’s bad news. You notice you chose your friends partly for what they could do for you. When it catches you unprepared like that, it does damage. It numbs people. It takes the beauty out of things that used to feel beautiful.

There are two stories about what people are like. The first is the one we tell children, and each other: people are kind, love is pure, friends are loyal, and we look after the weak. I’ll call it the fairy tale. The second is rarely said out loud, but it’s everywhere you look: people are selfish, competitive and quietly cruel. I’ll call it the animal.

The cynic says the fairy tale is a lie and the animal is the truth. I don’t think so. The fairy tale is real. The kindness, the love and the loyalty were built by the same evolution that built the selfishness. They aren’t a mask over the animal; they’re part of it, and they’re older than our ability to reason about them.

The trouble is that the fairy tale is fragile. It grew up long before the intellect did, and the intellect can kill it with one clever argument: you only love her because she’s beautiful. This essay is about facing the animal honestly, and then protecting the fairy tale from being killed by logic. Not by hiding from the logic, but by taking it further.

Two sides, one origin

You don’t need a biology degree for this. Evolution is one simple rule: things that copy themselves, copies that vary a little, and whatever gets copied more becomes more common.

Under that rule, a trait that makes you help anyone at your own cost doesn’t last. The help goes to people who mostly don’t carry the trait, so it gets copied less than the selfish version and fades. Helping only spreads when the help flows back to copies of itself: to family, who share your genes, and to people who’ll help you in return.

So evolution kept two kinds of survival in us. Your own, and your group’s. Both help the genes along, so we got both: a selfish side that looks after number one, and a social side that genuinely cares. Apes together strong. But not every ape, and not all the time.

You can see the edges of the social side in everyday life. In your own town you walk past hundreds of strangers without a thought. Meet someone from your town in another city, and you’re instantly on the same side. Meet them in another country, and you’d help them like a cousin. Our care is drawn in circles of familiarity, and the circles stretch and shrink.

The two sides often clash, and they clash inside the same head. Someone far below you is struggling, and you feel real sympathy. You want them to do better. Someone you know overtakes you, and you feel something else: part of you wants them to fail. There’s even a word for the pleasure of watching someone fall, schadenfreude. Helping the person below costs you nothing. The person passing you is a rival.

Or take the butterfly and the cockroach. Most people would feel bad crushing one and wouldn’t think twice about the other. Both are insects. One is pretty. Our kindness follows what’s close, cute and useful to us, not a principle.

So I don’t think we’re dark inside. The kindness is real; it’s built in as deeply as anything else. But we’re not only good inside, either. There’s a dark side sitting right next to the kind one, and we almost never talk about it.

Why don’t we see it? Partly because seeing your own selfishness clearly doesn’t win you anything, so a mind built to win things doesn’t bother. Partly because reasoning is a newer skill than feeling, and it looks at one thing at a time, so two contradicting feelings can share a head for a lifetime without ever meeting. And partly because we explain our feelings after the fact, flatteringly. We rarely say “I’m jealous”; we say “she got lucky”. (I wrote about that habit in What Is Ego?.)

When the intellect finds out

Now point the intellect at your closest relationships, honestly.

You didn’t befriend just anyone. You picked people around your level or a bit above: people who were fun, useful or impressive to be around. Love usually starts with attraction.

It helps to see where the choosing happens. Evolution built slots in us: for a mother and a father, for a partner, for friends. The longing for each was installed long before you were born. What differs is how many people can fill them. Almost anyone could be your friend, and plenty of people could be your partner, so you choose, and the choosing runs on tie-breakers: looks, status, usefulness. That’s where the give and take comes in.

Your parents’ slots were filled the day you were born, by the only people who could fill them. You loved them at the age of one, long before you knew what they did for you. There was nothing to choose, so there was no trade in this lifetime. The trade was made by evolution, over millions of years.

Seen this way, every relationship looks like a trade: some made in this lifetime, some made by evolution. That’s the moment the fairy tale is in danger. Am I only friends with him because he’s useful? Is all love just a deal? People defend themselves from that thought in one of two ways, and both cost them.

Ignorance. Don’t think about it. This is the easiest shield there is, and most people use it. But it protects you by hiding, not by making you strong. It works until the next crack, and there’s always a next crack.

Cynicism. Decide the fairy tale was a lie. It’s all give and take, people are selfish, the world is rotten. Call it the “fuck society” mindset; it’s one of the most common I see. It’s a toxic relationship to have with society. We’re social animals, so the person in this mindset needs society and hates it at the same time. And what they hate it for, selfishness, is something they share; they just can’t see it in themselves. The cynic’s mistake goes deepest with family. They take a love that evolution installed over millions of years and read it as a deal made in this lifetime, as if a one-year-old loved their mother for the meals. It leaks into everything else close, too. Every kindness looks like a move, every friendship like a deal that’ll end. You can’t relax into any of it.

There’s a third way, and it doesn’t need you to look away from anything.

Scaffolding

When a building goes up, it’s wrapped in scaffolding. You can’t build without it. But once the building stands, the scaffolding isn’t holding anything up. Leave it on, and all it does is hide the building.

The give and take is the scaffolding. It’s how relationships get built: two people with something to offer each other, close enough to keep meeting. While it’s up, something else grows inside it: the bond itself. Shared history, trust, being known, caring what happens to each other. Once that’s there, the reasons that brought you together stop mattering. They were the tie-breakers that got you started, not the thing holding you together. That’s why people go on looking after parents who can no longer give them anything.

The cynic looks at the scaffolding and thinks it’s the building. With friends and partners, the scaffolding was real, but it was only ever scaffolding. With family, there was hardly any to begin with: the building came ready-made.

So yes, the intellect is right about how it started. It’s wrong about what it became. The fairy tale doesn’t need protecting from the truth. It needs the rest of the truth.

Gaming evolution

Here’s the part I like most. Love evolved too. So did compassion, and so did the pleasure of seeing someone else happy. They were built to keep the group together and the genes moving. But once you see how they work, you don’t have to use them only the way evolution intended. You can use them on purpose, to get as much out of life as possible.

Love as a gift to yourself. If the value is in the building, you don’t need anything back. The feeling of loving someone is itself the reward, and it’s one of the best things a human body can feel. In that sense love is selfish: you’re enjoying a gift your own mind gives you. The other person is the vessel, and love is the nectar you get to drink from it. That isn’t a cold way to see it. It means your love doesn’t depend on what they do for you, which makes it unconditional. That’s how a selfish animal can love selflessly. You don’t have to stop being selfish. You only have to notice where the real reward is.

Compassion for everyone. The selfishness that makes people angry isn’t something most people chose. It’s the same machinery in all of us: the need to be seen, to feel important, to be safe. Someone who keeps praising themselves isn’t attacking you. They’re hungry for acknowledgement, the same hunger you have. Why not let them have it? It costs you nothing. Remember the circles of care: they grow with distance. Go far enough, and everyone is from your town.

And the cheapest pleasure there is: being happy that someone else is happy. You don’t even have to do anything. Buddhism has a word for it, mudita, joy at someone else’s joy, and treats it as the cure for jealousy. As far as I’m concerned, it’s the peak.

Hate is optional. The obvious objection: what about people who actually hurt you? Don’t you need to hate them to fight back? Krishna tells Arjuna to fight, but he never tells him to hate. You don’t need hate to know that being exploited is bad for you, or to decide what to do about it.

You’ll still get angry, and that’s fine. Think of anger as a smoke alarm evolution installed. It goes off by itself and it’s good at waking you up. But the alarm doesn’t put the fire out; you do. Once you’re dealing with the fire, the screaming doesn’t help. So let it wake you, deal with the fire, and switch it off. Anger is the alarm going off. Hate is leaving it ringing for years.

Someone tried to exploit you: that’s a point lost. If you now hate them, that’s one more point lost, and you gave this one away yourself. You’ve lost the ability to feel anything warm towards that person, and you carry it long after they’ve stopped thinking about you.

None of this means being a pushover. Keep what you feel separate from what you do. You can stop someone, cut them off or beat them in a deal, and still not hate them. The action follows your judgement; the feeling stays warm. It isn’t a favour to them. It’s a favour to you.

Keeping the fairy tale

You start life with a fairy tale you can’t look at too closely. Most people either keep it by never looking, or look once and lose it.

You can do better than both. Look at the animal, all of it. Then keep going: see the bond that grew behind the scaffolding, use love on purpose, choose compassion, let hate go. The fairy tale that comes out the other side is the same one you started with. The only difference is that it can’t be broken anymore. You haven’t hidden the dark side. You’ve covered it with love.

Hindu thought calls life lila, a play. Play your part, friend, rival or opponent, with your whole heart and without hate, and enjoy the play.
