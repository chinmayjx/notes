The Cost of Needing to Understand Everything

The Cost of Needing to Understand Everything

Oct 21, 20269 min read

  • essay
  • learning
  • thinking

On bottom-up thinking, the bad memory that isn’t, and learning to trust the box

Some people can open a textbook the night before an exam, skim it, and walk out with good marks. If you’ve always envied them and never managed it yourself, see whether any of this sounds familiar:

  • You can’t learn a fact until you know why it’s true. Being told isn’t enough; you need to see it derived.
  • Subjects you can rebuild from a few principles come easily. Subjects that arrive as a pile of facts and exceptions feel impossible.
  • You’ve read the whole theory of something and never actually used it.
  • Your notes never get going, because you don’t understand the topic well enough to write them yet.
  • You look at an architecture diagram, a page of boxes and arrows, and nothing sticks.
  • Somewhere along the way, you decided you just have a bad memory.

I was like this for most of my life. I want to describe how it works, what it costs, and the odd moment it flipped, because I don’t think the problem is memory at all.

A good observation turned into a law

Somewhere around class eight, I made myself a rule. If I studied something, I’d understand it to the core. Otherwise it was a waste of time: I’d read it, forget it, and be left with nothing.

There was something true in that. Deep understanding is the kind that lasts, and the kind you can build on. The mistake was turning a useful observation into a law. Once it was a law, my mind simply refused to work with information it didn’t fully understand.

Call this bottom-up thinking. You start at the foundations and build upward, and you won’t stand on any floor until you’ve personally checked every floor below it. Top-down thinking goes the other way. You start with the big picture, accept its parts as given, and dig into the details only where you need to.

What bottom-up costs

The main cost is the price of entry. Before I could reason about even basic things in a subject, I had to build a deep, concrete model of how it worked.

In school this sorted subjects for me in a way I didn’t understand at the time. Geometry was easy because I could see it; algebra took much longer. Vectors were fine because I could picture arrows moving. Matrices and determinants were a grey zone, because I couldn’t picture what a determinant was. Chemistry was a disaster. It’s full of rules with exceptions, facts you’re expected to hold without deriving them, and I couldn’t hold them. I concluded that my memory was bad.

The same pattern followed me out of school. For years I wanted to get into machine learning. I read Michael Nielsen’s Neural Networks and Deep Learning and understood it down to the mathematics of how a network learns. I never trained a single model. For someone who thought the way I did, working in machine learning would have meant doing a PhD first, because I couldn’t act on anything I didn’t fully understand.

That’s the real shape of it. It isn’t laziness, and it isn’t a bad memory. It’s an inability to act under uncertainty.

The workarounds

Software made the problem impossible to ignore. Nobody can understand a modern software stack to the bottom; there’s far too much of it. The whole field runs on abstraction. You use things whose source you’ve never read, and you trust that they do what they say.

My mind wouldn’t do that, so I found a way around it. When I met a system I didn’t have time to learn properly, I’d invent a hypothetical version of it out of building blocks I already understood: things I’d seen working in real code, mechanisms I could picture end to end. How might the internet move a page from a server to my screen? How might a regex engine actually match a pattern? How might Git store history? For Kubernetes, I’d imagine how I would build it myself from parts I’d used before. The guesses didn’t have to be right. They only had to be made of real things, so that my mind would accept them.

This can sound like abstraction, but it’s the opposite. An abstraction asks you to accept a component by what it does, without knowing what it’s made of. My hypothetical models did the reverse. I couldn’t accept anything my mind couldn’t trace down to concrete parts, so when I didn’t have the real mechanism, I made up one that was plausible. It was still bottom-up thinking, just with invented foundations instead of real ones.

It worked, more or less, and it gave me a solid base for my job. Depth wasn’t useless either: in the few areas I understood well, it sometimes let me see an unusual way through. But every new system still needed its own homemade model before I could reason about it, and every one of them cost a lot to build. I was never going to be the person who learns it the night before.

The flip

The change came from somewhere unexpected: I was trying to work out how to organise my notes.

Note-taking had always been hard for me, and now I can see why. You take notes before you understand a subject, while it’s still half-formed. I couldn’t. My instinct was that my notes had to mirror reality. They were meant to document how things actually are, in my own words, so I could look at them later and recall the mechanics. You can’t mirror something you don’t understand yet, so I was stuck.

Then I thought about a biology textbook. If it mirrored reality, it would need a separate chapter for every human being, because that’s how we exist: billions of separate bodies with a lot in common. A textbook doesn’t do that. It describes one model body, organised into parts, and inside each part it describes the variants. The chapter on blood covers group A and group B, though no one has both. The book isn’t a copy of anyone. It’s a structure of its own, built so you can make sense of any real body you meet.

That was the realisation. Notes don’t mirror reality. They exist in their own right. An abstraction, a model, a box on a diagram is as real as anything made of atoms. It’s real in a different way, because its job is to help you move through reality rather than copy it. (It’s the same idea as in Logic Isn’t About Truth: a model has to be useful, not a replica.)

This probably comes naturally to a lot of people. For me it was a gift.

What changed

The first thing that changed was the architecture diagrams. I’d never really been able to look at Kubernetes, and the problem was never the diagram. It was that I didn’t respect the boxes. I didn’t believe in a component until I’d rebuilt it in my head from real parts. Once I accepted that the box is the thing, at least at that level, the diagrams started to stick.

Which suggests the “bad memory” was never about memory. There’s a well-known study from 1973. The psychologists William Chase and Herbert Simon showed chess positions to masters and beginners for a few seconds, then asked them to rebuild the board from memory. The masters did far better, but only on positions from real games. When the pieces were placed at random, the masters’ advantage mostly disappeared. They didn’t have better memories. They saw the board as chunks, meaningful patterns they already knew, and they remembered the chunks. Memory needs something to hang on. If your mind won’t accept the hooks, nothing sticks.

Other things followed:

  • I could take notes on half the picture, knowing I’d revise them.
  • I could start using something before I fully understood it, and let the use teach me.
  • I could act on less information, which is most of what people mean by high agency. Games like chess and poker may reward the same habit: you reason with patterns and odds, never with the whole picture.
  • I could work in a team. As a bottom-up thinker I couldn’t have worked with ten people, because nobody can understand everyone’s work to the bottom. You reason with the components other people built, and you trust their edges.

Now I build abstract components, treat them as real, and reason with them entirely inside the model. I no longer force each one back down to something physical first.

Why some subjects felt illogical

Looking back, chemistry wasn’t illogical, and neither are biology, finance or most of the things I avoided. These subjects work with partial information and real uncertainty. Chemistry has exceptions because the underlying physics is far too complicated to work out from scratch every time, so it gets summarised in rules that mostly hold. Financial models never fully describe a market. The people in these fields aren’t being sloppy. They’re doing the only thing anyone can when there’s more information than one person can hold: build high-level models, use them, and amend them as they learn.

If you’re a bottom-up thinker, the way in looks something like this:

  • Start from the top. Learn the main parts and how they connect before learning any one part in depth.
  • Treat the boxes as real. A component you don’t understand inside is still something you can reason with.
  • Go deeper only where the top fails. If the high-level model explains what you see, leave it. Dig into the one place where it doesn’t.
  • Expect to revise. A first model is meant to be replaced. It isn’t a promise.

That’s all it took: a change in what I counted as real. It came while I was organising my notes.
