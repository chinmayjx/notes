The Fear of Attachment

The Fear of Attachment

Nov 02, 202612 min read

  • mind
  • spirituality
  • attachment

How “attachment is suffering” turns into action paralysis, and how to get your life back without giving up the insight

“Attachment is suffering” is one of the most widespread ideas I know. Buddhism puts craving at the root of all suffering. Hindu thought calls attachment moha, a kind of delusion, and treats detachment as a virtue. Jainism’s ideal is to own and cling to as little as possible. If you grew up anywhere near these traditions, you probably absorbed the idea without ever choosing it. I did.

It isn’t only a spiritual idea, either. You don’t need a soul or rebirth to reach it; you can get there by watching your own mind. (I wrote about that distinction in Spirituality vs Philosophy.) So this isn’t a piece against spirituality, and I’m not writing it from outside. It’s about what this one idea does to people when they take it halfway, and how to take it the rest of the way.

Why it looks so obviously true

The idea spreads because it really does match what you see. Wherever there’s suffering, there’s attachment. Most suffering is craving something you’re attached to: a person who left, a job you didn’t get, a body that’s getting older.

But turning up together isn’t the same as causing. Attachment is present at every scene of suffering. That doesn’t make it the culprit.

I think the more accurate statement is that delusion is suffering. Specifically, a lopsided kind: too much attachment to what you don’t have, and too little to what you already have. The grass is always greener on the other side. That’s the actual loop. You crave what’s over the fence, ignore what’s in your hands, get the thing over the fence, and then it’s in your hands and you ignore it too.

Look at the classic arguments again with that in mind. The Buddha’s “desire leads to suffering”. The hedonic treadmill, where every gain fades and you need the next one. Maya, the world as illusion. They’re all arguments against chasing mirages. They’re arguments against delusion. It’s easy to read them as arguments against attachment itself, and that misreading is where the trouble starts.

What happens when you believe it

Watch people who go deep into philosophy or spirituality and you’ll often see one of two things.

Some lose the ability to act practically. They drift away from ordinary life and end up only comfortable around people like themselves.

Others split in two to get by: a spiritual self for the weekend retreat and a working self for Monday. I don’t think being several people is a bad thing; I think we all are. But it means you can’t build on one consistent set of beliefs, because the beliefs you hold most deeply keep telling you that what your working self wants is a trap. And the two selves don’t stay neatly apart. One leaks into the other.

Practise “attachment is suffering” consistently and you arrive at action paralysis. You can’t plan anything long-term, because every long-term goal looks like a mirage. It will betray you. It will leave you suffering. Or you’ll get it, and it’ll leave you running after the next thing. So why put in years of work for it?

You never stop acting

Here’s the catch. You can’t actually stop. The Gita says it plainly: nobody can stay without acting, even for a moment; your nature makes you act anyway.

So the question is never whether you’ll act. It’s what will drive the acting. There are roughly three options:

You can act from reason: you look ahead, weigh things up, and plan. You can act from a routine: a fixed path that doesn’t need evaluating. Or you can act from impulse: whatever you feel like right now.

“Attachment is suffering”, taken halfway, switches off the first one. Reasoning about the future needs a future worth reasoning about, and you’ve just declared every future goal a mirage.

So people fall back on the second. This is partly why so many traditions come with ready-made routines: rituals, service, a monastery’s timetable, renunciation itself. They fill the day with action that doesn’t need reasons. That isn’t a criticism. If you’ve decided that planning is pointless, a fixed routine is a sensible thing to put in its place.

If there’s no routine, the third takes over. Your mind has disarmed you of the most powerful weapon you had, your intelligence. You can’t reason yourself into doing anything hard, because by your own beliefs nothing is worth it. So emotion and impulse drive instead. Here’s the irony: the person trying hardest to escape desire ends up the one most ruled by it. They don’t plan, so they indulge. They don’t commit, so they react.

The Gita has a word for this state: tamas, the inertia of dullness, laziness and indulgence, which it ranks lowest of the three qualities a person can be steered by. It’s close to pessimistic nihilism: nothing matters, so I’ll do whatever’s easiest. A very bad place to be.

Discipline gets very hard from there. Worse, the spiritual ideas start working as cover. “It’s all maya anyway” becomes the reason not to do the boring work that compounds over years. Psychologists call this spiritual bypassing: using spiritual ideas to dodge the hard parts of life rather than face them.

The spectre at the feast

That was about self-control. The same belief also numbs the good moments.

You’re eating an ice cream. You’re on a trip with friends. You’re having a genuinely good time. And somewhere at the back of your head there’s a killjoy: don’t enjoy this too much. You’ll get attached. It’ll end, and it’ll hurt.

The belief becomes the spectre at the feast, the guest who keeps reminding everyone that the party will end. You can’t fully sink into anything. You lose the ability to immerse yourself in what Hindu thought calls lila, the play of life.

Buddhist teachers saw this coming. They describe equanimity, the calm the practice is aiming for, as having a “near enemy”: indifference. From the outside the two look the same. Inside, one is peace and the other is numbness. The fear of attachment is how equanimity quietly turns into indifference.

The built-in fixes, and what they cost

The traditions aren’t unaware of the problem, and most come with a fix. The usual one is moderation: stick to the basics, don’t be overambitious, everything in moderation. That does give you enough motivation to look after the basics and keep going.

But it takes something out of you. Moderation covers what you need to get by. It has nothing to say to an ambitious person. If you want to build something big, there’s no room for that in “don’t want too much”.

There are exceptions. Karma yoga, from the Gita, says act fully but let go of the results. People do use it, selectively, to motivate themselves. It only goes so far when the rest of your beliefs still say the whole thing is a trap. You’re back to being two people.

So the fixes help. They don’t get you out.

Take the argument one more step

Here’s what gets you out, and it doesn’t need anything from outside these philosophies. It only needs you to go one step further than most people do.

If attachment is bad because it causes suffering, ask: what is suffering? It’s a feeling. An emotion, a chemical state, like any other. And if you’re so desperate to avoid it that you’ll give up enjoying ice cream, then you’re attached. You’re attached to not suffering, to being tranquil, to the idea of being free of attachment. You haven’t escaped attachment at all. You’ve just moved what it points to.

The same reasoning that told you attachment is the problem gets you here, if you keep following it. But the mind doesn’t keep following it, because it has no incentive to. It reaches “attachment is suffering”, gets the comfortable feeling of having understood something deep, and stops. And you’re left with the fear.

So take the extra step. If attachment causes suffering, then don’t be attached to avoiding suffering either. That’s what equanimity actually means: not running from pain, not running after peace. Accepting both. Once you get there, the fear of attachment has nothing left to stand on.

That deals with the fear. It doesn’t yet give you your life back. If you’ve spent years convinced that everything is meaningless, you have a few things to reclaim, and I’d go in this order.

First, reclaim the basics

Some pleasures are built into us so deeply that they’ve been there for as long as there have been humans. Being part of a good community; we’re social animals. Close relationships. Good food. A healthy body that moves. Being outdoors. Music. These are quick, reliable ways to calm your nervous system, wired in by evolution long before anyone wrote a scripture. And they’re available almost everywhere, from a tribe in Africa to a metro city in America.

None of these are permanent. Health can go, and so can people. But they’re different from the things that make attachment painful. Attachment hurts when you can’t feed it, and when its target keeps moving: the promotion, then the bigger one; this car, then that one. The basics don’t move. You don’t have to win them or compete for them, and most of them don’t depend on anyone’s approval. They’re always roughly within reach. Being attached to a walk in the evening isn’t delusion.

The traditions never asked you to give these up. Not really. The misreading did. So the first thing is to stop letting any belief, however deep, take away the basic animal enjoyment of being alive. Take a pause. Be in the present. Notice the gifts that are always there.

Then, keep the spiritual highs alongside

As you lean back into ordinary life, something will push back. Detachment has its own pleasures: the stillness after meditation, the lightness of letting go, the awe of feeling small in a big universe. A lot of people who are stuck in action paralysis are stuck partly because they don’t want to lose those. Leaning towards ordinary pleasures feels like a threat to them.

A voice will say: if you’re attached to the trivial things of life, how will you ever reach the spiritual highs?

Turn the question around. Why are you attached to the spiritual highs? They’re elusive precisely because you’re clinging to them. The same rule applies to them as to the ice cream.

It helps to see both for what they are. The basics are wired into the body’s oldest machinery. The spiritual practices are discoveries people made about that same machinery, like hacks for the nervous system: ways of reaching calm and awe that nobody knew about until someone tried. They work on the same body. They don’t compete.

You don’t need to settle on one consistent middle ground between them. You can enjoy the ice cream and the meditation the way you enjoy two different songs, without one cancelling the other. Let each come and go. Let the body flow. You can be several people, the one who loves a good meal and the one who sits in silence, as long as some part of you is keeping an eye on the whole.

Last, reclaim your ambition

Ambition is the hardest, because nihilism has the best argument against it. Measured against the universe, your startup, your career and your book don’t matter. That’s true.

But the part of you that knows it’s all meaningless doesn’t have to be in charge all the time. It’s right, and it’s useful to have around. It shouldn’t be the only voice, because when it is, it hands the wheel to impulse.

This is where two old ideas help. Karma yoga: act fully and honestly, and let go of the results. Lila: life as play, a drama you take part in for the sake of taking part. A good actor plays a villain with total commitment and doesn’t go home a villain. Play each of your roles honestly, the founder, the friend, the parent, without becoming attached to the role. The game is meaningless and you can still play it to win, the way you play anything for fun.

And use the insight in reverse, too. When you notice you’ve crossed from enjoying something into chasing it, that’s when “attachment is suffering” earns its keep. That’s the moment to remember maya: the grass looks greener from here, and this has stopped being attachment and become delusion. The insight is a tool you pick up when you need it, not a rule to live your whole life under.

Where this lands

Attachment was never the problem; delusion was. The idea that attachment is suffering is a good warning against chasing mirages. Taken halfway, it switches off your reason, hands your life to impulse, and puts a spectre at every feast. Taken all the way, it lets go of the fear too.

So reclaim the basics. Keep the spiritual highs without clinging to them. Play your ambitions like a game you care about and don’t need. You were going to act anyway. You might as well enjoy it.
