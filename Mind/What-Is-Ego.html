What Is Ego?

What Is Ego?

Oct 22, 202610 min read

  • mind
  • ego
  • psychology

One word for hurt pride and for what monks try to dissolve, a definition that covers both, and why a weaker ego buys you peace

We use the word ego in two places that seem to have nothing to do with each other.

The first is everyday. “He wasn’t invited to the meeting and his ego took a hit.” “Don’t stroke her ego.” “It’s an ego thing.” Here ego means something close to pride or self-respect: the part of you that wants to be acknowledged, and gets hurt when it isn’t.

The second is spiritual. People talk about ego death, about dissolving the ego, about the ego being what separates you from everything else. When the ego goes, they say, you dissolve into the universe, or into union with nature.

One sounds like office politics and the other like enlightenment. I think they’re the same thing. Pinning it down explains both uses, and explains why so many traditions tell you to make it smaller.

The I-maker

The spiritual meaning is older, so start there. In Indian philosophy the word is ahamkara, which literally means “the I-maker”. It’s the part of the mind that takes this body, and the thoughts and roles that come with it, and says: this is me. Most contemplative traditions have some version of the idea. The details differ, but the core is the same: ego is identifying with one particular person, the one in this body, as me.

On its own that sounds harmless. Of course I’m me. The trouble starts with what comes next.

A bias towards one person

Once you’ve identified with someone, you start favouring them. Here’s my definition:

Ego is a bias that tilts your thinking in favour of the person you identify as. I should be acknowledged. I deserve respect. I’m special. My idea is better. I wasn’t wrong.

Identifying comes first, and favouring follows. Psychologists have names for the favouring: self-serving bias (taking credit for your wins and blaming circumstances for your losses) and egocentric bias (overestimating how much you matter, and how central you are to what’s going on).

That one definition covers both uses of the word. When your ego gets “hurt” or “stroked”, the bias is being threatened or fed. When a monk talks about ego death, it’s the bias switching off: no particular person gets special treatment any more. Same thing, different volume.

Why call it a bias

A bias is anything that pulls your reasoning away from where the evidence points. Ego does exactly that.

Watch any conversation where egos are involved. It stops being a conversation. People use it to promote themselves, to fish for compliments, to cover up a mistake. Somebody tells a story and somebody else tops it. An argument that should end with “oh, you’re right” goes on for another twenty minutes, because conceding feels like losing. The question itself slips into second place, behind who comes out looking better.

So weakening the ego makes you more rational, almost by definition. Take away the bias and what’s left is the question.

But the traditions make a bigger claim: that a weaker ego makes you more at peace. That one needs an argument.

Why ego costs you peace

First, a concession. Ego does give you highs. When someone with a big ego gets praised, they really do feel great, maybe better in that moment than someone who doesn’t care much. So I won’t argue that a big ego makes you miserable all the time. My claim is narrower: it buys you a few moments, and costs you your baseline. Here’s why.

It’s a zero-sum game. Everybody wants to feel special. But special is relative: you can only be above average if someone else is below it. So it can’t satisfy everyone, and conversations turn into small contests over who’s smarter, more successful, more interesting. Even when you win today, everyone else is still playing.

It’s fragile, and other people feed it. The respect and acknowledgement ego lives on are controlled by somebody else. A word can hurt it. A missing word can hurt it: you weren’t thanked, you weren’t invited, nobody laughed at your joke. Carrying an ego is like walking down a crowded street carrying something made of glass. Every so often it catches the light and it’s lovely. Most of the time you’re just watching for elbows. That constant low hum of vigilance is the opposite of peace. And the bigger the ego, the bigger the glass, and the more people who might bump into it.

The amplifier is lopsided. People feel a loss about twice as strongly as an equal gain; psychologists call this loss aversion. Ego turns up the volume on both. But turning up a scale that’s already tilted makes the tilt bigger. (The tall dashed bar is the other half of the story; more on it below.)

The two sides don’t last the same, either. Praise fades within days: you get used to it, and you need more. The threat of being disrespected never fades, because it can come from anyone at any time. Like most of the drives evolution gave us, ego was built to give you a direction, not a destination. It isn’t meant to be filled.

The research points the same way. Psychologists call self-worth that hangs on approval and results contingent self-worth, and people with more of it tend to have more stress and anxiety, and sharper swings up and down.

Isn’t the high worth something?

Say you weaken your ego. You do lose the particular thrill of having it stroked. Does that matter?

Cocaine produces real pleasure too, and nobody thinks you’re missing out by not taking it. It isn’t even a special kind of pleasure. It’s the same feeling, reached by a different route, with a heavy price attached. The ego high is like that. The pleasure at the end is the ordinary kind. You don’t care whether your parcel came by postman or by drone; what matters is that it arrived. If you can get there by a route that doesn’t involve carrying glass through a crowd, you’ve lost nothing.

And the ego’s pleasure has a low ceiling. The best it can offer is feeling a bit more special than the monkey next to you. Now look at what people report from the other end: the moments when the sense of self drops away completely, in deep meditation, in awe, in prayer. They describe them as among the most intense experiences of their lives. Meditators reach for drug words to describe them, and people on psychedelics reach for spiritual words; in clinical studies, the part volunteers most often single out is the dissolving of the self. Whatever you think of those states, the point stands: a smaller ego doesn’t cap your highs. Satisfying your fragile monkey ego is what has the low cap.

And you don’t need a monastery for the everyday version. There’s a lot of pleasure on the other route.

What you get instead

With a weaker ego, the world starts to look like a circus. The petty games we play, the status fights, the outrage of the week, people competing over who’s busiest. It’s very funny, once you’re not anxiously taking part.

Watch a documentary about chimpanzees: the alliances, the displays, the sulking when someone lower down gets the better spot. Then look at an office, a family group chat, a comment section. Then, and this is the important part, look at yourself. When you can see your own version of the chimpanzee display and laugh at it, something lets go. Laughing at yourself is one of the most rewarding things a person can do, and it’s only available when your ego isn’t guarding the door.

There’s also a whole category of suffering that’s hard to even produce without ego. Try being humiliated without a self that matters. Try envy, or wounded pride, or resentment that someone didn’t give you your due. Each of these needs a “me” that’s important. Not all sadness is like this: grieving someone you love isn’t ego. But a lot of the everyday sadness we argue ourselves into runs through “this happened to me, and I deserved better”. Weaken the ego, and those arguments stop being convincing.

Life starts to look less like a test and more like an amusement park.

Weaker, not gone

There’s a reason I defined ego as favouring yourself rather than identifying with yourself.

If ego were the identifying, then getting rid of it would mean no longer knowing which body is yours. Some meditative and spiritual states do go there, and dissolve the sense of self completely. They can be profound, but you can’t run a life from inside one. You need to know whose hands to move and whose rent to pay.

Favouring is different. You can keep “I” as a plain label, a pointer to this particular monkey, and still act, think, plan, want things and work hard. What you drop is the extra weight: the sense that this monkey is special, that everything is a verdict on it, that the stakes are always enormous. You keep the ability to act. You lose the ability to make everything feel serious and daunting.

It isn’t all or nothing, either. You aren’t one fixed self, the same in every moment. There’s the version of you that’s competing in a meeting, the one that’s relaxed with old friends, the one sitting quietly in the morning. Some carry more ego than others. The aim isn’t to make every one of them egoless. It’s to turn the volume down on average, so the version that shows up most often is carrying less glass. Think of an orchestra rather than a soloist: lots of players, some louder than others, and the job is to make the music good, not to silence everyone. (I’ve written about this way of seeing the self in Microframeworks, but you don’t need it here.)

Where this lands

Ego is the I-maker plus a bias: first you take this person to be you, then your thinking tilts in their favour. That tilt is what gets “hurt” in the office and what “dies” in the monastery.

Its highs are real. But it’s a zero-sum game, played with something fragile, fed by other people, and tuned to feel losses more than gains. Turning it down costs you a few small peaks, gives you back your baseline, and opens up highs the ego can’t reach. It also gets you the best seat in the circus, including for the act where you’re the clown.
