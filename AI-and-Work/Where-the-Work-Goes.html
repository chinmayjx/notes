Where the Work Goes

Where the Work Goes

Oct 21, 20269 min read

  • essay
  • ai
  • work

If AI can’t end scarcity, it moves it. Two spectrums for guessing where.

In Why the Age of Abundance Won’t Feel Like One I argued that AI can end a lot of scarcity, but not wanting. If that’s right, work isn’t going away. It’s moving, the way it always has.

“Computer” used to be a job title. Rooms of people did calculations by hand, all day. Machines took that work, and the people moved on to other work. Nobody today misses being a human calculator.

So the interesting question isn’t whether work survives AI. It’s where it goes. I use two spectrums to think about that. Neither is a prediction. They’re more like a compass.

First, where the leftover scarcity lives

Once food, shelter and health are taken care of, what’s left is mostly the positional economy: wanting to stand out from the people around you. A better car than your neighbour’s, a nicer wedding than your cousin’s, more respect in your office.

This kind of scarcity isn’t limited by resources. It’s limited by people. The anthropologist Robin Dunbar found that across primates, the size of the social group tracks the size of the neocortex. For humans that works out to roughly 150 people we can keep real relationships with. That’s the circle you compare yourself to.

That circle doesn’t grow when factories get faster. You will always be part of a small group, and you’ll always measure yourself against it. Production can flood the world with stuff. It can’t flood the thing that’s actually scarce, which is standing out within your circle.

Keep that in mind, because both spectrums lead back to it.

Spectrum one: from utility to art

New technology starts out competing on usefulness. A faster phone, a waterproof watch: pay more and you get more. Even status is tied to utility at this stage. The rich have the better thing because it is better.

Then technology makes the useful thing so cheap that everyone has it. The utility race is over, but the wish to be different from everyone else isn’t. So scarcity gets brought back artificially, through brand, craft, design, limited editions, and sometimes just the price itself.

Watches are the clearest case. Cheap, perfectly accurate watches arrived decades ago, and now your phone tells the time anyway. Telling time is basically free. The watch market didn’t die. It moved to the far left of this spectrum, where a watch is mostly a statement about who you are. You can watch the same thing happen with clothes, where cheap and decent became the norm and logos took over, and with phones, where nearly every phone is good enough and which one you carry still says something.

Art moves too

You might think art is the next thing AI takes over. But art itself has always followed this pattern.

Before cameras, a lot of painting was valued for accuracy: a portrait that really looked like the person, a landscape that looked like the place. Then photography made accurate images abundant. The craving for beauty didn’t go away. Its target moved, toward impressions, abstraction, and eventually modern art with its strange lines and shapes. Duchamp once put a signed urinal in an exhibition and called it art. I honestly can’t tell you why it’s art, and that’s my point. Art moved to where the scarcity was: novelty, a message, a context, culture, sometimes politics. Being different became part of what makes something art.

So when AI can make any image in any style on demand, art doesn’t end. It moves again, toward what instant abundance can’t supply: a particular person, a story, a context, a reason it exists.

Not just old things with an art sticker

I don’t mean the future economy is today’s products with a luxury label on them. A lot of existing things will go that way, but there will also be new kinds of goods, and they’re hard to imagine in advance.

Handicraft is a good example from the past. Once machines made goods cheap, handmade things became more valuable, not less. They carry cultural context, and they’re scarce because human hands are scarce.

People already complain that modern buildings are bland and lifeless next to the colourful, ornate buildings of the past. That complaint is the same pattern again: we look for novelty wherever scarcity still exists.

Or take a stranger one. If one day everyone has an AI companion, a human partner might become a status symbol. Some people might even put up with a difficult relationship partly because of what it signals. I don’t know if that will happen. I’m less interested in guessing the products than in the desire underneath them, which doesn’t change: to be seen, and to be different.

Spectrum two: from tech to product to sales

This one sounds corporate, but it isn’t. Look at almost any system that involves people and you’ll find the same layers.

At the bottom is implementation: the real building blocks. Bricks for a house, code for software, hands and labour for most things.

Above that is product: deciding what to build, and coordinating the people building it.

At the top is the part that makes the system visible to the world: selling it, building trust, keeping relationships alive. The social side of a system sits at the top because coordinating people and reaching people is what the lower layers exist to serve.

A household, a temple, a startup and a government all have these layers. So this isn’t really about companies. It describes any system with people in it, which is nearly all of them.

The bottom layer has always run away

Technology has been eating parts of the bottom layer forever. Each time, the people doing that work ran sideways into a different kind of implementation. Human calculators became programmers. Farm workers moved into factories, and factory workers into offices. It never happened that every kind of implementation was taken at once, so there was always somewhere sideways to go.

That’s what’s different now. AI isn’t taking one kind of implementation. It’s coming for the layer.

People say physical work like welding and plumbing will be safe for a long time. Maybe. But given the pace of robotics and energy, I’m not betting on it. I also don’t need to, because the bottom isn’t the only place people work. Every layer of a system has people in it. So don’t run sideways. Run up.

Can AI take the top too?

The obvious reply is that AI will do sales as well. Sales bots will flood every inbox.

They probably will. But the top layer runs into the same limit as the positional economy: human attention. People can only keep up so many relationships. That’s Dunbar again. A lot of what happens at the top is like a keep-alive signal: staying in touch, showing up, slowly earning trust, being someone people actually know. AI has no motives of its own. It always acts on someone’s behalf. A flood of bots doesn’t increase the number of people anyone can hold in their head. If anything it makes the few slots in that head more valuable.

Which raises the question: if everything is abundant, what’s being sold up there? The answer goes back to spectrum one. It’s whatever the scarcity has moved to: craft, art, culture, status, and human attention itself.

What I do about it

So what if my job is the kind that’s likely to disappear?

I use the two spectrums to decide what to get better at. On both of them, I move left: toward art and the human side, and up toward the social layer. I’m an introvert, and I’m deliberately working on the social side anyway: psychology, communication, networking, art, culture. As the non-human work gets automated, being more human becomes the valuable part.

Learn in a language that travels

The other thing I do is about how I learn. I’ll write about this properly another time, but here’s the short version.

If you store what you learn in the vocabulary of one system, like physics formulas or the syntax of one programming language, it stays in that system. If you store it in a more abstract language, it travels.

Abstract algebra is the best example I know. It doesn’t study numbers, or shapes, or matrices. It studies a group: any set of things with a way of combining them, as long as four simple rules hold. Combining two things gives you something still in the set. The order in which you group the combinations doesn’t matter. There’s one element that changes nothing. Every action can be undone. Prove a theorem about groups once, and it holds at the same time for clock arithmetic, for rotating a square, for matrices, and for the moves on a Rubik’s cube.

There’s a catch. The abstraction must not strip away the usefulness. If you drop any of those four rules, you can prove almost nothing interesting. Too abstract and you know nothing; too concrete and you know one thing. The skill is finding the right level.

When you find it, things start connecting. A lesson from physics shows up in philosophy. What a relationship teaches you about people turns into empathy you can use in product or sales.

That isn’t a coincidence. Every one of those domains runs on the same substrate: the same kind of brain, with a small set of desires and well-understood ways of acting on them. The patterns repeat because the thing producing them is the same.

AI will take a lot of work, and the scarcity will move to where the humans are: standing out, making things that mean something, being trusted by a few people who know you. So move up, move toward the human, and learn in a way that travels.
